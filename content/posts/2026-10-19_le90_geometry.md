DOTA labels a ship with eight corner coordinates. A detector cannot regress eight numbers that still have to form a rectangle, stay ordered, and survive a flip. [oriented-det](https://github.com/DL4EO/oriented-det) trains and exports a rotated box as five numbers — center, width, height, angle — under one rule, **le90**. Every Hub slug that ends in `_le90_` is that rule. This post is what the rule actually does.

## Five numbers, and why they are not unique

An `RBox` is `(cx, cy, width, height, angle)`.

The image origin is the top-left corner. **+x** points right. **+y** points down. The angle is radians. A small **positive** angle swings the width edge from +x toward +y. On the screen that is down and to the right, so the box looks clockwise. The same rotation matrix is counter-clockwise when y points up, which is why the geometry notes still say “mathematical counter-clockwise.” Training does not use that y-up helper. It uses the image-space flips and rotates in `oriented_det.data`, then writes the box back to le90.

A rectangle does not care which way is the bow. Turning it by 180° is the same pixels: `(w, h, θ)` and `(w, h, θ+π)` draw one box. Swapping the sides and adding 90° is the same pixels again: `(w, h, θ)` and `(h, w, θ+π/2)`. A regression head that is free to emit either form spends capacity on a tie. le90 cuts the tie.

## The rule

`normalize_le90` does two things, in this order:

1. Fold the angle into **[-π/2, π/2)** by steps of π. A half-turn does not swap the sides.
2. If width is shorter than height, swap them and add π/2, then fold the angle back into the same interval.

After that, **width is the long edge** (or either edge, when the box is square), and the angle is the heading of that long edge. The interval is closed on the left and open on the right. A vertical long edge is **−90°**, not +90°. Both `+π/2` and `−π/2` land on `−π/2`.

Two parameter vectors for one 80×20 box at 30°:

| Stored before normalize | After `normalize_le90` |
|---|---|
| width 80, height 20, angle +30° | 80 × 20 at **+30°** |
| width 20, height 80, angle +120° | 80 × 20 at **+30°** |

The docstring case is the vertical one. `RBox(100, 100, 50, 100, π)` — short side called “width,” angle 180° — becomes width **100**, height **50**, angle **−π/2**.

Loaders call this on the way into the batch. Flips and random rotate call it again on the way out. A checkpoint and a label file only line up when both sides have been through the same function.

## From eight DOTA coordinates

A DOTA line is a quad, then a class, then a difficult flag:

```text
x1, y1, x2, y2, x3, y3, x4, y4, category, difficult
```

The corners walk the perimeter. They are not required to be a perfect rectangle, and they are not required to start on the long edge. The loader builds a `QBox`:

- The polygon is wound counter-clockwise in the shoelace sense (positive area with the usual formula). On an image, that vertex order is the one the library calls counter-clockwise.
- The first vertex is the top-most point. A tie breaks to the left-most.
- The angle is `atan2` of that first edge.
- Width is the mean length of that edge and the edge opposite it. Height is the mean of the other pair.

A skewed quad becomes a rectangle at that step. The original eight coordinates are not what the loss sees. Then `normalize_le90` runs, so the long edge is width even if the quad’s first edge was the short one.

HRSC XML `mbox` angles, FAIR1M polygons, SSDD corners, and HRSID polygons take the same path. HRSID outlines with more than four points become a minimum-area rectangle first, then le90. The [HRSID note](/posts/2026-10-01_hrsid_sar_ship_benchmark/) is that conversion; this post is the angle rule underneath it.

## Flips have to keep the long edge

A horizontal mirror sends `θ` to `−θ`. A vertical mirror sends `θ` to `π − θ`. Both can push the angle outside [-90°, 90°) or leave the short side in the width slot, so the flipped box is normalized again.

The diagonal flip is the one that does **not** touch the angle. MMRotate mirrors the center and returns. oriented-det used to apply `π − θ` there. That is a real heading error on every diagonally flipped ship, and it was fixed before the v0.3 zoo. The [v0.3.1 note](/posts/2026-09-21_oriented-det_v0_3_0_four_datasets_and_onnx/) records the patch. If you implement your own aug outside `oriented_det.data`, copy `flip_diagonal`: move `(cx, cy)`, leave `θ`, then `normalize_le90`.

`geometry.transforms.rotate` is the other trap. It is rbox math in a y-up frame. Rotating a PIL image with that angle, without negating it, turns the pixels one way and the boxes the other. The training collate uses `apply_rotate_to_image` / `apply_rotate_to_rboxes` and normalizes afterward.

## What does not belong in a le90 slot

**OpenCV `minAreaRect`.** Its angle and its choice of which side is width are a different convention, historically in a range near [-90°, 0°]. Pasting `(width, height, angle)` from that call into a DOTA recipe rotates the box by a quarter turn on some headings. Convert the four corners through `QBox`, then `normalize_le90`.

**le135.** MMRotate also defines a long-edge box whose angle sits in [-45°, 135°). oriented-det recipes and Hub weights are le90. A le135 checkpoint is not a drop-in for `odet train`.

**Bow versus stern.** le90 identifies a rectangle, not a directed heading. A ship at 20° and the same ship labeled 200° are one box. If the product needs a vector, that is another head.

## Where you meet it

The string `le90` in `oriented_rcnn_dota_le90_1x` and in `rotated_rtmdet_dota_le90_3x` is this convention, not a dataset version. Regression targets, the decoded boxes in `predictions.json`, and the pre-NMS ONNX graph all speak it. The [ONNX walkthrough](/posts/2026-10-08_onnx_export_without_pytorch/) consumes those boxes with a rotated NMS in Python; the angle in the graph output is still le90. [v0.4.0](/posts/2026-10-10_oriented-det_v0_4_0_rotated_rtmdet/) uses the same five numbers.

Square-ish objects hide the swap. Ships, harbors, and bridges do not. If two overlays disagree by about 90° and the long side has swapped, you are looking at a missed `normalize_le90`, not at a bad detector.
